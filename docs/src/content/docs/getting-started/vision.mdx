**Anyone can fix a page they are reading, and the person who owns that page keeps the file.** Cyberbaser is the layer that makes both true at once: a stranger edits on the web, the change comes back as clean markdown in a vault its owner controls, and the owner approves it without having to be a developer. The long arc is that those vaults link to each other, so a wiki is one web among many rather than one site standing alone.

## The original dream, restored

It was written down in a vault note in April 2024, before any of this existed, and [mined back out in 2026](/cyberbaser/agent-context/zz-log/2026-04-13-vault-vision-mining/):

> Go to someone's Obsidian website, click "edit page", and immediately edit the content right there. Click submit, and it generates a PR and runs GitHub Actions. You can also edit by cloning the repo into Obsidian or from GitHub directly, so 3+ ways to edit. You can "edit with someone" for live anonymous collaborative editing. Each page shows contributors with photos and a contributors page. Obsidian community plugins can run in multiple environments, not just in Obsidian locally, but also in GitHub Actions, serverless functions, and client-side CMS apps.

Some of that shipped. Some was decided against for a written reason. Some simply stopped being mentioned, which is the failure mode this ledger exists to end.

<details class="cbv-more">
<summary>The full archaeology: how each piece moved, and the two commits where things went missing</summary>

Two moments account for nearly every gap above, and neither of them was a decision.

**April 2026, the numbering collapse.** The research plan's ten numbered tasks were flattened into unnumbered bullets on a "this is over-structured" note. Reasonable on its own, but the numbers were also the handles, and six tasks lost theirs. Cross-links in the mining logs still point at anchors that no longer exist.

**July 2026, the tidy-up.** A commit whose message was about aligning the knowledge base to recent decisions deleted four open research tasks with no decision record: the publish-site survey, the data-sync and media-at-scale strategy, the SEO baseline, and **operational model and sustainability**. The last of those was added by the maintainer deliberately, in its own commit, and its text is a paraphrase of the question that triggered this whole alignment pass eleven weeks later. All four are [restored to the roadmap](/cyberbaser/getting-started/roadmap/#reopened-research-deleted-in-error-2026-07-25-restored-2026-07-26) with provenance.

The rule that came out of it: a knowledge-ops pass may re-point, re-word, or re-file an open question. It may not delete one. Deleting an open question requires a decision entry that states the answer.

**On plugin execution, one thing the kill never engaged.** Obsidian shipped an official CLI and a headless client in 2025 and 2026, and the mining log noted at the time that this "potentially dissolves the hardest problem". A later audit confirmed community plugins do execute through the CLI, but established that it is inter-process control of a running desktop app rather than a runtime, and that the headless client excludes plugins. So the kill stands, but for the coupling reason rather than the difficulty reason. The record should say that the hardest problem partly solved itself and we still declined it.

</details>

<details class="cbv-more">
<summary>Things that arrived that were never in the dream, including one product decision a dependency quietly reversed</summary>

Built, and not asked for in 2024:

- A **default-deny publish boundary**. Nothing leaves the vault without an explicit grant, with leak tests. It caught 587 stale publish flags before they published personal journals.
- A **projection** step with case-collision guards and frontmatter pre-flight.
- An **Obsidian-syntax validator** that classifies a change as clean, suspect, or damaging, so a tool that corrupts markdown is detectable rather than merely regrettable.
- A **link validator** the renderer refuses to be, run as a ratchet against a frozen baseline: broken internal links went from 1145 to 779, and the worst class from 248 to 7.
- A **trust classifier** that reads its policy from the base branch, so a proposed change cannot promote its own author.

Bought for free by adopting a commodity renderer: sitemap, RSS, per-page social images, alias redirects, backlinks, hover previews, and search. That is the hub-and-spoke architecture working exactly as intended, and nobody ever wrote it down as a win, which is why it reads as drift instead.

One thing to correct rather than celebrate: **graph view was deliberately deferred** on the only usability evidence available, which said it is a writer's tool that confuses readers. The renderer's default layout ships it anyway. A reasoned product decision was reversed by a dependency's defaults, and nobody noticed either the reversal or the free wins that came with it.

</details>

## Three things the vision was missing

### The maintainer must not need git either

[Principle 3](/cyberbaser/getting-started/principles/#3-contributors-shouldnt-need-to-learn-git) says contributors should not have to learn git. Nothing ever said the same about the **person standing up the wiki**, and everything built so far assumes that person has a GitHub account, a repository, three CI workflows, branch protection, and CI logs as their only way to see what happened. That is not "knows git", it is "is a developer who runs infrastructure", and it is a higher bar than the one Principle 3 exists to remove.

<details class="cbv-more">
<summary>The three honest ways to answer it, and what constrains all three</summary>

The gap is recorded; the direction is open and belongs to the maintainer rather than to a research pass. Three candidate answers:

1. **A hosted service.** Bring your vault, get a contributable wiki. Someone else operates the forge and the pipeline. This was already named as a candidate value centre on the roadmap before that task was deleted.
2. **A one-click self-host bundle.** A forge plus the pipeline in a single container, on a box or a small VPS the maintainer controls.
3. **A local-first application** that syncs to storage the maintainer already has, with no forge in the picture at all.

[Principle 1](/cyberbaser/getting-started/principles/#1-a-single-source-of-truth-you-own) constrains all three the same way: they still need storage they own. The source-of-truth research already scoped git as "the current manifestation, not the essence, with a documented off-ramp", so this is anticipated rather than contradictory.

An earlier internal conclusion, that no hosted console is needed because the forge is the interface, is **sound only for a maintainer who has a forge and can operate one**. It holds for the dogfood case and does not generalise. Reopened.

**Proposed as a peer of Principle 3, not a footnote:** *neither side needs git*. That is a change to the principles page and is not made unilaterally here.

</details>

### The thing being rebuilt is the wiki

The comparison that matters is not "another static site generator". It is **the wiki**: a page anyone can improve. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Introduction_to_editing) demonstrated at planetary scale that reading and fixing can be the same surface, that no account need be required to fix something, and that **moderation rather than identity** is what keeps it safe. None of that requires a developer platform, and the people who will make wikis do not have GitHub accounts.

<details class="cbv-more">
<summary>What the modern wiki keeps from Wikipedia and what it changes</summary>

**Keep**, because it is proven: open editing as the default, review rather than gatekeeping as the safety mechanism, trust earned through contribution history, and a stable citable URL for every page.

**Change**, because the incumbent stack shows its age. [MediaWiki](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/MediaWiki) is over twenty years old and self-hosting it means running a database and a PHP application; the content lives in database rows in a bespoke markup, so leaving is an export project. Cyberbaser's answer is the inverse: the content is plain markdown files in a folder you own, the software is a set of small pieces rather than one monolith, and the renderer is deliberately replaceable. If the whole layer disappears, the wiki is still a folder of files that opens in any editor.

This is also the honest scope line. "Wikipedia replacement" means **the mechanism**, the thing that makes a wiki contributable, and not the encyclopedia. The dogfood content happens to be a security vault; the target is anyone who would otherwise have stood up a wiki.

Related reading: [The problem](/cyberbaser/concepts/problem/) · [Contribution on your terms](/cyberbaser/concepts/problem/#contribution-on-your-terms)

</details>

### A web of webs where you control your web

One wiki is a dead end. The value shows up when independently owned bases can **reference, curate, annotate, compare and fork each other** without a central Cyberbaser database: your wiki cites a page in mine, an ordinary meta-wiki publishes a collection containing both, another meta-wiki disputes its mapping, and nobody gains authority over anyone else's files. Each base keeps its own host, publication state, history, rights and contribution rules.

<details class="cbv-more">
<summary>What was already researched, what it would take, and the honest status</summary>

The earlier 83-system survey looked at [Nostr](https://nostr.com/), [ActivityPub](https://www.w3.org/TR/activitypub/), the [AT Protocol](https://atproto.com/), IPNS, [Ward Cunningham's Federated Wiki](http://fed.wiki/), Matrix, and decentralized identifiers. Its durable finding still holds: **git has a structural zero on federation**, and changing the source of truth does not remove the need for a connection layer.

The protocol sketch from that survey does not hold. The current [federation research](/cyberbaser/research/federation/) rejects `did:web + custom /.well-known/ manifest + commit-hash harvest` as the spine. The replacement is **federation by publication**: ordinary direct HTTPS links form the durable floor; each base publishes its own descriptor, public inventory, outbound Linksets, revisions, rights and capabilities; ordinary meta-wikis publish source-qualified collections, mappings, annotations and licensed mirrors; and every crawler, graph database, cache and search provider is a disposable view.

That shape permits recursion and disagreement without a central Cyberbaser database. A meta-wiki can contain another meta-wiki, fork its collection, dispute its mapping, disappear, or be replaced. None receives naming, merge, moderation or truth authority. Signatures and digests can prove limited facts about bytes; they do not prove truth or permission.

**Status, stated plainly:** the architecture is researched and the next falsification task is active, but no network feature is built and no R-number locks it. The controlled prototype uses three subject wikis, two competing meta-wikis, two differently ranked search providers and one deletable cache. It is scoped to run locally now rather than wait for an outside operator; an independently implemented peer is still required before calling the profile interoperable.

Full survey: [Is git the right source of truth?](/cyberbaser/research/source-of-truth/) · design sketch: [Federation](/cyberbaser/research/federation/) · original brief: [Challenge 09](/cyberbaser/agent-context/zz-challenges/09-source-of-truth-alternatives/)

</details>

## What this page promises that does not exist yet

Earlier versions of this page said cyberbaser "does not build the trust and moderation stack from scratch, it composes existing tools" while also promising a rendered preview before approval and approval from a phone. Both cannot be true. Nothing on the shelf provides those two things, so either they get built or they stop being promised.

The reconciliation, stated once so it stops drifting: **compose where a commodity exists, build where none does.**

## Where the identity might sharpen

:::caution[A proposal, not a change. The locked one-liner is unchanged until the maintainer says otherwise.]
The locked identity is **"an interoperability layer for contributable, version-controlled knowledge bases."** Three things on this page press on it:

1. **"Contributable" currently means the reader.** It does not say the maintainer is also spared git, and that turned out to be the higher bar.
2. **"Version-controlled" reads as "git",** which the research explicitly scoped as the current manifestation rather than the essence. The word may be describing an implementation.
3. **Nothing in it says the knowledge bases connect to each other,** which is the long arc.

Three options, in ascending order of how much they disturb what is locked:

- **A. Keep the line, add a second sentence.** *"Neither the reader nor the maintainer needs git."* Cheapest, relitigates nothing, and fixes the gap that actually bit.
- **B. Keep the line as the internal definition, add a public one.** Something like *"the modern wiki: anyone can improve it, you own the files, and it can link to other people's."* The technical line stays for design work; the positioning line goes on the homepage, which still leads with older wording.
- **C. Amend the line.** *"An interoperability layer for contributable knowledge bases you own, and a protocol for linking them."* Swaps a manifestation for the essence and adds federation, at the cost of reopening a locked decision.

**Recommendation: A plus B.** Option C is a real argument and should not be settled by an agent.
:::

## The same wiki from four sides

<details class="cbv-more">
<summary>Reader, vault-native contributor, zero-git contributor, maintainer: what each one actually experiences</summary>

These are journeys, not features, and the examples deliberately vary. Swap in a research group's notes, a company handbook, a course, or an open standard, and the story is the same. The security vault is the first content dogfooded, not the scope.

**The reader.** A student lands on a linear algebra page from a search result. It loads fast, reads like a well-kept wiki, has cross-links and a last-updated date that matches reality, and carries an "Edit this page" link at the bottom. They copy a paragraph and move on. They neither know nor care that it came from someone's Obsidian vault.

**The vault-native contributor.** A specialist notices a detection technique missing from a page, opens the vault in Obsidian, adds a callout, wikilinks it to the overview page, commits, and pushes. The site rebuilds and the change is live. What must never happen: the link graph breaking, the callout rendering as plain text on the web, or the wikilink getting rewritten on the way through.

**The zero-git contributor.** An HR lead reads the onboarding page in their company handbook and spots an outdated reference. They fix it in place. What happens next is the maintainer's setting: on an open wiki it submits anonymously, on a tighter one they verify an email first. Either way it lands in a queue rather than going straight live. They never clone anything and never learn git. **Today this person's only route is the forge's web editor, which needs an account, so this journey is the vision rather than the present.**

**The maintainer.** Changes arrive in a queue. The trust curve they set when they stood the wiki up decides how much scrutiny each one gets: anonymous edits queue heavily, verified contributors get faster review, long-trusted contributors edit directly. The maintainer never leaves their own editor as the primary authoring surface, and web contributions flow into the same file they edit by hand, with no parallel system to keep in sync. **Measured, honestly: the loop currently takes three to five minutes, not the sixty to ninety seconds an earlier draft of this page claimed.**

</details>

<details class="cbv-more">
<summary>A day in the life, when it is working. This is the target, not a description of today.</summary>

On an ordinary Tuesday, three things happen without anyone coordinating.

**09:14.** A reader in Berlin finds a typo in an open standard's spec page and fixes it in place, anonymously. It lands in the queue with a preview attached. The maintainer approves it on their phone during coffee.

**11:47.** A consultant in Austin pushes three new pages from their own vault, fully cross-linked to existing content. The build deploys and the pages are searchable on the wiki.

**16:22.** A graduate student opens a change request through the forge with one-line fixes across four pages. They have never used Obsidian. It passes the checks and merges.

None of these people know the others exist. None of them had to work out which entry point was the "correct" one. All three produced equally good edits to the same files.

Of those three, the middle one works today end to end. The first needs the anonymous endpoint and the preview. The third works but requires an account.

</details>

## What this deliberately does not do

<details class="cbv-more">
<summary>Anti-features and v1 non-goals, with the reason attached to each</summary>

- **It does not host your vault.** It reads from a repository you own. If cyberbaser disappears, the vault still opens.
- **It does not replace the maintainer's editor.** Local authoring stays wherever the maintainer already works. The web surfaces exist for people who do not have that setup.
- **It is not a security tool.** It is general purpose. The security vault is the first content dogfooded, and earlier drafts had this backwards.
- **It does not do real-time collaborative editing.** Reassigned to a private sibling project rather than deferred to an unspecified later.
- **It does not solve in-vault search.** Site search is whatever the renderer provides. Searching your own notes stays in your own editor.
- **Comments and discussion threads are out of scope for v1.** The forge's issue tracker is the fallback.
- **Heavy identity verification is post-v1.** The trust curve ships; know-your-customer style verification does not.
- **A diffing interface on the reader-facing site is out of scope for v1.** Git history is the fallback.

One former non-goal has been promoted to an open question rather than a "no": **paid hosting or a hosted service**. It is the most plausible answer to "who operates this for a maintainer with no forge", and the research task that would have decided it was deleted by accident and has been [restored](/cyberbaser/getting-started/roadmap/#reopened-research-deleted-in-error-2026-07-25-restored-2026-07-26).

</details>
